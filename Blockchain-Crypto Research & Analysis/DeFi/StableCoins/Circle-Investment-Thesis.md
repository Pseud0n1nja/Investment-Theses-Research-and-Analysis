## 2. Business Overview

### 2.1 What Circle Is

Circle Internet Group, Inc. is the issuer of USDC — the world's second-largest stablecoin ($76B supply) and the largest *regulated* stablecoin globally. The company was founded in 2013 by Jeremy Allaire and Sean Neville, and went public on the NYSE in June 2025, raising $459M in primary proceeds at a $31 per share IPO price. After reaching a peak of $299 during its first weeks of trading, the stock has since corrected to ~$55, driven by fears of rate cuts and competitive entry.

But reducing Circle to "a stablecoin issuer" fundamentally mischaracterises the business. Circle is building what I would call a **full-stack internet financial platform** — a three-layered architecture that looks more like Visa for the blockchain era than a simple treasury management operation:

**Layer 1: Blockchain Networks (Infrastructure)** — Circle's newly launched Arc blockchain, a purpose-built Layer 1 for payments, FX, and capital markets. Arc offers sub-second settlement finality, an integrated FX engine, opt-in privacy controls, and uses USDC as native gas. Over 100 organisations (including banks like Standard Chartered, HSBC, and Goldman Sachs, plus payment players like Visa and Brex) are participating in the testnet, with commercial launch planned for 2026.

**Layer 2: Stablecoins and Tokenized Assets (Products)** — USDC (dollar stablecoin, $76B), EURC (euro stablecoin), and USYC (yield-bearing stablecoin). These are the "products" that travel on the blockchain rails.

**Layer 3: Applications and Utilities (Distribution)** — Circle Payments Network (CPN), Cross-Chain Transfer Protocol (CCTP), programmable wallets, and developer tools. These are the on-ramps that connect the traditional financial system to the blockchain infrastructure.

**Revenue Model:** Approximately 96-99% of revenue comes from *reserve income* — the interest earned on U.S. Treasuries and cash backing USDC deposits. The remaining 1-4% (growing rapidly) comes from transaction-based fees across blockchain integrations, CCTP, CPN, and USYC. Critically, Circle shares a substantial portion of reserve income with distribution partners — primarily Coinbase (~50-55%) and Binance (upfront fees + ongoing incentives) — to drive adoption.

### 2.2 USDC: How Issuance and Redemption Works

The mechanics of USDC are deceptively simple, and understanding them is essential to understanding Circle's economics:

**Issuance:** A customer (individual, institution, or exchange) deposits U.S. dollars with Circle. Circle holds those dollars in segregated reserve accounts — currently invested in short-term U.S. Treasuries (managed through BlackRock's Circle Reserve Fund) and cash held at regulated bank custodians. Circle then mints an equivalent amount of USDC tokens on the customer's chosen blockchain. The customer now holds USDC that is redeemable 1:1 for U.S. dollars at any time.

**Redemption:** The reverse process. A customer sends USDC back to Circle, Circle burns the tokens, and transfers the equivalent U.S. dollars to the customer's bank account. Standard redemptions clear within 1-2 business days; instant redemptions (for a fee) settle in minutes.

**The Float Economics:** While USDC is outstanding, Circle earns the yield on the underlying reserves. **Reserve Composition (Q3 2025):** Approximately 80% in short-duration U.S. Treasuries via the Circle Reserve Fund (USDXX), and 20% in cash held at major banking institutions. This is intentionally conservative — USDC's reserves are 100% in cash and short-term Treasuries, compared to Tether's more diversified (and less transparent) reserve portfolio that historically included commercial paper, secured loans, precious metals, and Bitcoin.

Understanding *where* USDC sits is just as important as understanding *what* it is. Circle's distribution strategy operates across four channels, each reinforcing the others:

Binance, signed as the first designated Stablecoin Ecosystem Partner in November 2024, holds ~12% of USDC supply. The deal involved a $60M upfront fee plus ongoing monthly incentives. Binance's integration has been transformative: USDC on Binance grew from 4% of total supply in 2023 to 14% by H1 2025. OKX, the third major exchange partner, further extends Asian distribution.

**Channel 2: DeFi and On-Chain Applications (34% of USDC supply)**

USDC is the dominant stablecoin in decentralised finance, holding >50% share in DeFi protocols. It serves as the preferred collateral in lending platforms (Aave, Compound, Fluid), trading pairs on decentralised exchanges, and the settlement currency for cross-chain transfers via CCTP. The DeFi channel is particularly valuable because it drives organic, sticky demand with no revenue-sharing requirements — USDC held in DeFi protocols generates full-margin reserve income for Circle.

**Channel 3: Circle On-Platform (14% of USDC supply, growing)**

A quietly important trend in Q3 2025 was the rise of USDC held directly on Circle's own platform — from 10% in Q2 to 14% in Q3. **Channel 4: Payments and Financial Services (<1% today, the growth story)**

The pipeline of 500+ institutional prospects suggests this channel will scale meaningfully over the next 2-3 years, particularly as Arc blockchain enables programmable payments with sub-second finality.

## 3. Category Primer: Stablecoins

### 3.1 What Stablecoins Are and Why They Exist

A stablecoin is, at its core, a tokenised representation of fiat currency living on a public blockchain. The concept is simple: an issuer takes custody of real-world dollars (or euros, or pounds), holds them in reserve, and issues digital tokens pegged 1:1 to that currency. The holder of the token can transact with it on the blockchain — send it, receive it, use it as collateral, earn yield on it — and can always redeem it back to fiat.

Why does this matter? Consider the plumbing of the current financial system. When I send $1,000 from India to the United States via a bank wire, that transaction traverses a chain of intermediaries: my bank → correspondent bank → SWIFT messaging → clearing house → receiving correspondent bank → recipient's bank. Each intermediary takes a cut, introduces latency, and operates on different ledgers that need reconciliation. Settlement takes 1-5 business days. The total cost for a typical cross-border wire is $25-50 per transaction, or 5-10% for smaller remittances.

Now consider the same transfer using stablecoins: I convert INR to USDC on an exchange → send USDC directly to the recipient's wallet on the Solana or Ethereum blockchain → recipient converts to USD on their exchange or keeps the USDC. Total time: 1-30 seconds. Total cost: $0.01-0.10. No intermediaries, no reconciliation, no settlement risk. The ledger *is* the settlement.

This is not theoretical. USDC transaction volumes reached $3T in just H1 2025, roughly 10% of SWIFT's total annual volume, for a token that is barely 7 years old. Monthly active stablecoin addresses have increased to 51 million (from 3 million in 2020). The combination of scalable blockchain infrastructure (Ethereum Layer 2s now settle transactions in 1-2 seconds at 1-2 cents per transaction) and regulatory clarity (the GENIUS Act) has sparked a fundamental re-evaluation of stablecoins' role in the global financial system.

### 3.2 Current Usage Split

Stablecoin usage today remains predominantly crypto-native, but the composition is shifting. Based on my analysis of on-chain data and Bernstein's estimates for Q2 2025:

**Crypto Capital Markets (~56% of total stablecoin supply):**
- Centralised exchanges (Coinbase, Binance, etc.): ~39% of supply
- On-chain DeFi applications (DEXs, lending, yield): ~17% of supply
- Primary use: trading pairs, collateral, settlement between crypto assets

**Wallet-to-Wallet Transfers (~39% of supply):**
- On-chain payments, transfers, and wallet balances
- Includes cross-border peer-to-peer transfers
- Growing adoption in emerging markets (Africa, Latin America, SE Asia)

**Issuer On-Platform (~5% of supply):**
- Held by stablecoin issuers for liquidity provision and payment services

**Payments and Financial Services (<1% today):**
- Cross-border B2B payments, remittances, stablecoin-native neo-banking
- Nascent but growing: CPN's $3.4B annualised GPV is the leading indicator

For USDC specifically, the mix is even more crypto-concentrated: ~78% in crypto capital markets (44% on exchanges, 34% in DeFi), ~16% in wallet-to-wallet, and the remainder on Circle's platform. This crypto-heavy concentration is both the near-term risk (crypto bear markets reduce demand) and the long-term opportunity (the 78% to ~54% shift toward payments represents massive incremental TAM).

### 3.4 Why "Stablecoin Rails" Matter vs. Cards/ACH/Wires

The comparison between stablecoin rails and existing payment infrastructure reveals why this is not a marginal improvement but a structural disruption:

| Parameter | Wire / SWIFT | Card Networks | ACH | Stablecoin (USDC) |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|-----|-------------------|
| Settlement Time | 1-5 days | 1-3 days | 1-3 days | Seconds to minutes |
| Cost per Txn | $25-50 | 1.5-3.0% | $0.25-1.00 | $0.01-0.10 |
| Operating Hours | Business days | 24/7 authorisation, batch settlement | Business days | 24/7/365 |
| Cross-Border Friction | High (correspondents) | Moderate (FX markups) | Domestic only | Native (blockchain is borderless) |
| Programmability | None | Limited | None | Full (smart contracts) |
| Transparency | Low (opaque reconciliation) | Moderate | Moderate | Full (on-chain, auditable) |

The cost advantage alone is compelling. For a $10,000 cross-border B2B payment, a SWIFT wire costs $25-50 plus FX markup (often 1-3%). The same transfer via USDC costs cents. Even at scale, stablecoin rails offer 90-99% cost savings on cross-border transactions.

But cost is only part of the story. The *programmability* of stablecoins enables entirely new financial products: automated escrow, streaming payments (pay by the second rather than by the month), conditional transfers (release funds when goods clear customs), and composable financial applications that stack on top of each other. Traditional payment rails cannot do this because they were designed as message-passing systems (SWIFT is literally a messaging protocol from the 1970s), not as programmable money.

## 4. Regulation and Market Structure

### 4.1 Regulatory Backdrop and Timelines

The regulatory landscape for stablecoins has shifted from headwind to tailwind, and the velocity of change is remarkable. Here is the current state across key jurisdictions:

**United States — GENIUS Act (Signed July 2025)**

The GENIUS Act (Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins) is the first major piece of digital assets legislation in the U.S. and arguably the most consequential regulatory development for Circle. Key provisions:

- **Three categories of issuers:** (i) Subsidiaries of insured depository institutions (banks); (ii) Federally qualified non-bank issuers (approved by OCC); (iii) State-qualified issuers.
- **100% reserve requirement:** Backed by U.S. Treasuries, repos, or demand deposits. Independent attestation required.
- **Foreign issuer compliance:** Offshore stablecoins must demonstrate technological capability to comply with U.S. requirements. This effectively creates a compliance barrier for Tether's existing USDT.
- **Non-financial company prohibition:** Public companies not predominantly in financial services cannot issue stablecoins without extraordinary approval. This is the provision that forces Amazon, Meta, and others to partner with existing issuers.
- **Payment stablecoin classification:** Treated as digital cash — not a deposit, not a security, not a commodity. Eligible as cash-equivalent margin for capital market clearing.
- **AML/KYC requirements:** Banking Secrecy Act compliance, including authority to block sanctioned entities.

The strategic intent is explicit: bring stablecoin innovation *onshore* to the U.S. The GENIUS Act recognises that stablecoins have become systemically important for the U.S. — more than 99% of fiat stablecoins are backed by the U.S. dollar, and stablecoin issuers are already among the top 20 holders of U.S. Treasuries. By promoting dollar-backed stablecoins, the Act reinforces dollar dominance in the digital economy and creates additional demand for Treasuries.

**European Union — MiCA (Markets in Crypto-Assets)**

MiCA came into full effect in December 2024 and establishes a licensing framework for stablecoin issuers in the EU. Circle has been proactive — it obtained an Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licence in France and launched EURC (euro stablecoin) within the MiCA framework. While EURC is small today, it positions Circle as one of the few issuers with regulatory approval in both the U.S. and EU.

**Other Key Jurisdictions:**
- **UK:** Financial Services and Markets Bill includes stablecoin provisions; Circle is engaged with the FCA.
- **Singapore:** MAS Payment Services Act provides a licensing pathway; Circle holds a Major Payment Institution licence.
- **Japan:** Revised Payment Services Act allows stablecoin issuance by registered entities.
- **UAE/Dubai:** VARA (Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority) framework; Circle has relationships in the region.

*Sources: GENIUS Act legislative text; MiCA regulation; Bernstein Primer (Oct 2025), pp. 4-5; SEC filings.*

### 4.2 Implications for Issuers: Onshore vs. Offshore

The GENIUS Act creates a sharp dividing line between regulated, onshore stablecoin issuers and offshore, less-regulated operators. This bifurcation has profound competitive implications:

**Onshore (Circle, Paxos):** Full regulatory compliance, transparent reserves, institutional trust. The tradeoff is cost: Circle bears significant compliance expenses ($263M in compensation alone in 2024, much of it compliance-related) and shares economics generously with distribution partners. The benefit is access to the U.S. market, institutional clients, and future bank/BigTech partnerships.

**Offshore (Tether):** Tether's USDT ($179B supply, 69% market share) dominates globally but operates from El Salvador with limited regulatory oversight. Tether recently announced USAT, a GENIUS Act-compliant stablecoin co-issued with Anchorage for the U.S. market. However, USAT starts from zero — it needs to build distribution, liquidity, and exchange integration from scratch, a multi-year effort. Tether's existing USDT may face access restrictions in the U.S. market over time, creating a potential share shift toward compliant alternatives like USDC.

The key insight: post-GENIUS Act, the stablecoin market bifurcates into (a) a high-compliance, high-trust onshore segment where Circle has a commanding lead, and (b) a more commoditised offshore segment where Tether continues to dominate emerging markets. Circle does not need to "beat" Tether globally — it needs to win the regulated, institutional, payments-oriented segment, which is where the growth is.

### 4.3 Implications for Banks/PSPs/BigTech Adoption

The GENIUS Act creates a clear decision tree for potential stablecoin adopters:

**Banks:** Can issue their own stablecoins under the Act. However, two structural barriers limit the threat to Circle: (1) Banks prefer permissioned environments — their stablecoins would likely be restricted to qualified institutional investors initially, lacking USDC's permissionless liquidity across 28 blockchains and DeFi protocols. (2) Stablecoins are fully reserved, making them poor substitutes for fractional-reserve checking deposits. Banks are more likely to use stablecoins for specific use cases (cross-border treasury management, trade finance) rather than as a broad consumer product.

**Payment Service Providers (Visa, Mastercard, Stripe):** These companies are adopting stablecoins as a settlement layer within their existing networks, not launching competing stablecoins. Visa has partnered with Circle to enable USDC settlement. Stripe acquired Bridge (cross-border stablecoin payments infra) and Privy (digital wallets), and is building the Tempo blockchain — but even Stripe's approach is "build with stablecoins" rather than "build a stablecoin." Circle's infrastructure (CCTP, CPN, Arc) makes it the partner of choice for PSPs wanting stablecoin capability without the regulatory burden of issuance.

**BigTech (Amazon, Meta, Walmart):** The GENIUS Act's prohibition on non-financial company issuance is unambiguous: these companies cannot issue stablecoins without clearing an extremely high regulatory bar. If they want to offer stablecoin-based payments (and they do — reports of Amazon and Walmart exploring stablecoins surfaced in 2025), they must partner with a regulated issuer. Circle's existing relationships with Shopify (via Coinbase/Stripe USDC integration) and its brand reputation position it as the natural partner.

### 4.4 Second-Order Effects

**Treasury Demand:** If stablecoin supply reaches $4T by 2035E (Bernstein estimate), and issuers are required to hold 100% reserves in U.S. Treasuries, that represents an additional $3-4T of Treasury demand. For context, Japan — the largest foreign holder of U.S. Treasuries — holds ~$1.1T. Stablecoins could become the single largest class of Treasury buyers outside the Fed. This dynamic aligns U.S. government interests directly with stablecoin growth, making regulatory reversal politically untenable.

**Compliance Costs as Barrier to Entry:** The GENIUS Act's compliance requirements (BSA/AML/KYC, reserve auditing, attestation, OCC oversight) create meaningful fixed costs. Circle has already built this infrastructure. A new entrant must invest tens of millions upfront before minting a single token — a significant deterrent, especially when the incumbent (Circle) has a 7-year head start and established institutional relationships.

## 5. Competitive Landscape

### 5.1 Direct Stablecoin Competitors

**Tether (USDT) — $179B supply, 69% market share**

Tether is the elephant in the room. With $179B in supply, USDT dwarfs USDC ($76B) and dominates emerging market usage. But Tether's strengths are also its vulnerabilities in the post-GENIUS Act world:

- *Offshore domicile:* Tether relocated from Hong Kong to El Salvador in 2023 — not exactly a regulatory gold standard. USDT's reserve composition has historically been less transparent (at one point including commercial paper, bitcoin, and precious metals, though Tether has since shifted primarily to Treasuries).
- *USAT launch:* Tether's response to the GENIUS Act — a new U.S.-compliant stablecoin co-issued with Anchorage. But USAT starts from zero, competing against USDC's $76B of established liquidity, 28-blockchain coverage, and exchange integrations. The cold-start problem for a new stablecoin is brutal: traders and institutions don't use a stablecoin because it's new, they use it because it has deep liquidity and wide acceptance. Building this takes years, not months.
- *Revenue model:* Tether retains 100% of reserve income (no revenue sharing). This makes Tether enormously profitable ($7.7B in H1 2025 alone) but also means Tether has never needed to build a distribution partnership model. In the regulated U.S. market, where exchange partnerships and bank integrations matter, Tether/USAT will need to adopt Circle's playbook — likely including revenue sharing — eroding its margin advantage.

**Paxos (PYUSD + USDG) — ~$3.3B combined supply**

Paxos is Circle's most credible domestic competitor, operating with a similar compliance-first approach. Its two key products are:

- *PYUSD (PayPal):* White-label stablecoin for PayPal with ~$2.5B supply. Integrated within Venmo with active rewards for holders. PYUSD has seen strong growth in DeFi integration. However, it faces the "cold-start problem" — PayPal's user base is large but not crypto-native, and PYUSD's 13-blockchain coverage lags USDC's 28.
- *USDG:* Backed by a consortium including Robinhood, Kraken, Mastercard, Anchorage, Galaxy, and Bullish. Supply is only $0.8B. The distribution partner list is impressive on paper but hasn't translated into meaningful supply growth yet.

**Ripple (RLUSD) — $0.8B supply**

Ripple, traditionally focused on its native XRP token for cross-border payments, entered the stablecoin market late. Its recent acquisition of Hidden Road (institutional crypto prime brokerage) provides a distribution channel, but RLUSD is sub-scale and XRP's volatility as a payment medium has historically been a liability rather than an asset.

*Sources: CoinGecko; Company disclosures; Bernstein Primer (Oct 2025), pp. 8-10.*

### 5.2 Indirect Competitors

**Bank-Issued Stablecoins**

Major U.S. banks with their enormous customer bases are potential stablecoin issuers under the GENIUS Act. JPMorgan already operates JPM Coin (now renamed Kinexys Digital Payments) for institutional transfers. However, bank stablecoins face structural limitations:

1. *Permissioned vs. permissionless:* Banks will almost certainly restrict their stablecoins to qualified clients operating within the bank's ecosystem. This means a JPM stablecoin can't be used on DeFi protocols, can't be bridged across 28 blockchains, and can't serve as a universal settlement currency. It's a closed-loop solution — useful for intra-bank settlement, but not a competitor to USDC's open network.

2. *Deposit cannibalisation:* Stablecoins are fully reserved (100% backed). Banks operate on fractional reserves — for every $100 of deposits, they lend out $85-90. A bank stablecoin literally transfers deposits from a high-yielding (for the bank) fractional reserve structure to a zero-earning (for the bank) full reserve structure. This is a net negative for bank economics. Banks will likely limit stablecoin usage to specific cross-border use cases rather than broad consumer adoption.

**Card Networks (Visa, Mastercard)**

Rather than competing with stablecoins, the major card networks are integrating them. Visa processes USDC settlements and has built analytics capabilities for on-chain monitoring. Mastercard is a partner in Paxos' USDG consortium. The card networks' approach is to add stablecoin rails as another settlement option within their existing networks — complementary to Circle, not competitive.

**Stripe / Tempo**

This is the most interesting emerging threat. Stripe's acquisitions of Bridge (cross-border stablecoin payments) and Privy (digital wallets), combined with its announcement of the Tempo blockchain, make it the closest thing to a potential direct competitor in the payments layer. However, Stripe is building *infrastructure for using stablecoins*, not a competing stablecoin itself. Its approach is "build the pipes" rather than "issue the currency." This makes Stripe both a potential partner (as demonstrated by the Coinbase/Shopify/Stripe USDC integration) and a potential "frenemy" if Tempo eventually favours a non-USDC stablecoin for settlement.

### 5.3 Distribution Competition

The real competitive battleground is not stablecoin issuance — it's *distribution*. The question isn't "who can issue a compliant stablecoin?" (many can), but "whose stablecoin becomes the default across the most platforms, chains, and use cases?"

Here, Circle has three distribution advantages:

1. **Exchange lock-in:** Coinbase and Binance together represent 34% of USDC supply. These relationships are contractual (revenue sharing), embedded in product integrations, and reinforced by years of co-development. A competing stablecoin would need to pay even more to prize USDC's position — a costly proposition.

2. **DeFi network effects:** USDC's >50% share in DeFi means it has the deepest liquidity pools, the most trading pairs, and the widest collateral acceptance. DeFi protocols are composable — each new integration builds on existing ones, creating exponentially more use cases. A new stablecoin has to convince hundreds of independent DeFi protocols to integrate it simultaneously.

3. **Multi-chain breadth:** 28 blockchains. Each blockchain integration requires engineering investment, security audits, and ongoing maintenance. USDC's multi-chain coverage is the broadest in the market, and Circle's subscription revenue ($24M in Q3 alone, +33% QoQ) from blockchain integrations is both a revenue stream and a competitive barrier.

### 5.4 Where Competition Bites: Pricing, Revenue Share, Incentives

Circle's Achilles' heel is its distribution cost structure. The Coinbase deal — while strategically essential — means Circle retains only 45-50% of gross reserve income. Each new partner deal (Binance's $60M upfront + ongoing incentives, OKX, future bank partners) further compresses the gross margin.

Here's the arithmetic of the margin squeeze: Bernstein estimates Circle's net reserve margin (reserve income after distribution costs) falling from 39% in 2024 to 35% by 2027E. This decline is the *cost of growth* — Circle is buying market share by sharing economics with distribution partners. The bet is that the volume gain (5x supply growth) more than offsets the margin compression (400 bps).

The risk scenario is one where a well-capitalised competitor (say, a Tether-backed USAT or a bank consortium) offers exchange partners an even more generous revenue share, forcing Circle to match at the expense of its already-thin gross margins. I view this as a real but manageable risk: Circle's existing liquidity and integration depth create meaningful switching costs that make it expensive for exchanges to switch primary stablecoins.

## 6. Operating Model and Unit Economics (Key Section)

### 6.1 Revenue Engine: Reserve Base × Yield

Circle's revenue model is deceptively simple in structure but rich in its driver decomposition. The formula at the highest level:

> **Gross Reserve Income = Average USDC Supply × Reserve Yield Rate**

But the real insight lies in decomposing each variable:

**Average USDC Supply** is driven by:
- End-of-period USDC in circulation (currently $76B, targeting $220B by 2027 year-end)
- Growth trajectory within the year (supply typically builds through the year, so the average is below the end-of-period figure)
- Category mix: exchange deposits (~44%), DeFi (~34%), wallet-to-wallet (~16%), Circle on-platform (~14%)

**Reserve Yield Rate** is a function of:
- SOFR rate (the primary benchmark for short-term Treasury yields)
- Portfolio composition (duration, mix of T-bills vs. repo vs. cash)
- Reinvestment lag (when rates change, the portfolio yield adjusts with a delay as instruments mature and are reinvested)

For 2024, the maths were: ~$34B average supply × 5.0% yield = $1.66B reserve income.

For my 2027E base case: ~$173B average supply × 2.6% yield = ~$4.6B reserve income.

The key insight is that supply growth is a *multiplicative* driver while rate changes are *additive*. A 5x increase in supply paired with a 40% decline in yield still produces a 3x increase in revenue. This is why I fundamentally disagree with the bearish narrative that "rate cuts kill Circle" — they don't, so long as supply keeps compounding.

**Other Revenue ($29M in Q3 2025, $316M 2027E):**
- Blockchain integration subscription fees: $24M in Q3 (+33% QoQ), earned each time Circle enables USDC on a new blockchain or layer. Currently 28 chains, growing.
- CCTP transaction fees: From cross-chain USDC transfers. Q3 volume: $31.3B (+90% QoQ).
- CPN transaction fees: From the Circle Payments Network. $3.4B annualised GPV, 5 months into launch.
- USYC fees: From Circle's yield-bearing stablecoin.
- Instant redemption fees: Premium for immediate USDC-to-USD conversion.

The margin on other revenue is remarkable: 99% gross margin in Q3 2025 (only $0.4M in associated costs on $29M revenue). This is essentially pure software fee revenue — zero variable cost. As this line scales, it provides both EBITDA growth and a natural hedge against rate cuts.

*Sources: Bernstein Financial Model; Company Q3 2025 filings; Bernstein Primer (Oct 2025), pp. 17-19.*

### 6.2 Distribution Costs: Who Takes What

Understanding Circle's distribution cost structure is essential for modelling the P&L and assessing competitive risk. There are three layers:

**Layer 1: Coinbase (~50-55% of gross reserve income)**

The Coinbase Stablecoin Ecosystem Agreement, restructured in August 2023, has two components:
1. Coinbase retains 100% of the float income on USDC held on the Coinbase platform (~22% of USDC supply in Q3 2025, down from 23% in Q2).
2. Coinbase receives 50% of the *remaining* reserve income after Circle's and other partners' payments.

Net effect: Coinbase captured ~50% of total USDC reserve income in Q3 2025 (down from 52% in Q2 — a positive directional trend). In dollar terms, Coinbase earned roughly $355M from USDC reserve income in Q3 alone.

**Layer 2: Binance ($60M upfront + ongoing incentives)**

The November 2024 deal made Binance the first designated Stablecoin Ecosystem Partner:
- $60M upfront fee (already expensed)
- Ongoing monthly incentives linked to USDC balances and marketing activity
- Two-year term with a one-year tail for obligations if terminated

Binance holds ~12% of USDC supply. The deal has been successful: USDC on Binance grew from 4% of total supply (2023) to 14% (H1 2025), making the incentive payments a high-ROI distribution investment.

**Layer 3: Other Partners and Future Deals**

Circle and Coinbase jointly decide on onboarding additional partners under the Stablecoin Ecosystem Agreement. OKX is an active exchange partner. Future deals with banks, PSPs, and BigTech under the GENIUS Act will likely involve similar revenue-sharing structures. This is the "cost of growth" — each new partner increases distribution (driving supply) but compresses gross margins.

**Net Reserve Margin Trajectory:**

However, the margin stabilises around 33-35% by 2026-27E as the Coinbase share of total USDC declines (from 55% of reserve income in 2024 to ~46% by 2027E) and higher-margin Circle on-platform USDC grows.

### 6.3 Net Revenue and Margin Drivers

Circle's profitability metric of choice is **Revenue Less Distribution Costs (RLDC)**, which is essentially gross profit. RLDC captures the net economics after paying distribution partners.

The RLDC margin story is nuanced: the *reserve income* RLDC margin is declining (39% → 33%), but the *total* RLDC margin benefits from the scaling of high-margin other revenue (95-99% margin). By 2027E, I estimate total RLDC margin at ~37-38%, down only 200 bps from 2024 despite the massive expansion of the distribution partner base — because the other revenue mix effect partially offsets the reserve income margin compression.

In dollar terms, RLDC grows from $659M (2024A) to $1,838M (2027E) — a 2.8x increase that powers the entire EBITDA expansion.

### 6.4 Opex Structure and Operating Leverage

Below the RLDC line, Circle's operating expense base is remarkably fixed relative to the growth in revenue:

| Expense Category | 2024A | 2025E | 2026E | 2027E |
|-----------------|------:|------:|------:|------:|
| Compensation | $263M | $855M* | $444M | $498M |
| G&A | $137M | $165M | $205M | $240M |
| IT Infrastructure | $27M | $35M | $41M | $47M |
| Marketing | $17M | $24M | $32M | $46M |
| D&A | $51M | $69M | $71M | $74M |
| SBC | $50M | $533M* | $150M | $168M |

*Note: 2025E compensation and SBC are elevated due to IPO-related one-time charges (stock-based compensation for pre-IPO equity conversions). Normalised 2026-27E comp levels reflect the actual ongoing cost structure.*

The key observation: total cash opex (ex-SBC, ex-D&A) grows from ~$444M in 2024 to ~$831M by 2027E — roughly 2x growth. Meanwhile, RLDC grows from $659M to $1,838M — roughly 3x. This differential is the operating leverage, and it's why EBITDA margin expands from 43% (2024) to 69% (2027E) despite gross margin compression.

The operating leverage is structural, not cyclical: Circle's platform costs (compliance infrastructure, blockchain integrations, developer tools) are largely fixed. Serving $220B of USDC doesn't require proportionally more compliance officers, more blockchain node operators, or more customer support than serving $44B. The marginal cost of an additional dollar of USDC supply is essentially zero.

### 6.5 Sensitivities

The two most important sensitivity variables are SOFR rate and average USDC supply. Bernstein's sensitivity matrix (which I consider the single most useful analytical framework for CRCL) shows:

**Revenue Sensitivity (2027E):**
- At $173B avg supply and 2.9% SOFR: $4.6B reserve income (base case)
- Per 25 bps rate change at $173B supply: ~±$433M revenue impact (~9%)
- Per $25B supply change at 2.9% SOFR: ~±$725M revenue impact (~16%)

**EBITDA Sensitivity (2027E):**
- At $173B avg supply and 2.9% SOFR: ~$1.3B EBITDA (base case)
- Per 25 bps rate change at $173B supply: ~±$152M EBITDA impact (~11%)
- Per $25B supply change at 2.9% SOFR: ~±$407M EBITDA impact (~30%)

The asymmetry is clear: EBITDA is 3x more sensitive to supply changes than to rate changes. Each $25B of incremental supply is worth roughly three 25-bps rate cuts in EBITDA terms. This is because distribution costs scale with supply (partially offsetting the revenue gain), but so does the operating leverage — the fixed cost base is spread over a larger revenue pool.

Key balance sheet observations:
- **Net cash position:** ~$2.7B as of the latest estimates (post-IPO proceeds), providing substantial flexibility for acquisitions, partner incentives, or Arc blockchain development.

- **Payments companies (Visa, MA, FI):** These are fee-based businesses with 50-70% operating margins and decades of distribution moat. Circle's revenue model (float income) is fundamentally different, and its margins are still maturing.

## 9. Risks and Mitigants

### 9.1 Macro Risk: Rate Cuts and Drawdown Behavior

**The Risk:** Circle's monoline revenue model (96-99% reserve income) means the P&L is directly linked to interest rates. Each 25 bps rate cut reduces 2027E revenue by ~9% and EBITDA by ~11%. If the Fed cuts aggressively to below 2% by 2027 (not the base case but possible under recession scenario), revenue could fall 30-40% below base estimates.

**The Mitigant:** Three structural offsets reduce the actual downside:

1. *USDC supply acts as a natural hedge:* Rate cuts typically accompany risk-on environments that drive crypto asset prices higher, which in turn increases demand for stablecoins. The 2020-2021 period demonstrated this — when the Fed cut rates to near-zero, stablecoin supply exploded from $5B to $150B. A low-rate environment that crushes Circle's yield could simultaneously drive a supply supercycle.

2. *Operating leverage compresses the sensitivity at the EBITDA level:* Because opex is fixed, EBITDA margins expand as supply grows, partially offsetting rate-driven revenue declines. In Bernstein's bear case ($170B avg supply, rates below 2%), 2027 EBITDA is $668M — still a 33% CAGR from 2024.

3. *Non-float revenue provides an income floor:* At 99% gross margin and $110M+ annualised run-rate (and growing 20%+ QoQ), other revenue provides a rate-insensitive earnings base that continues to compound regardless of the interest rate environment.

**Drawdown Behaviour:** CRCL stock has high beta to both crypto sentiment and interest rate expectations. From its $299 peak (June 2025) to ~$55 today (February 2026), the stock declined ~80% — driven by a combination of: (a) rate cut fears, (b) crypto market cooldown, (c) IPO lock-up expiry selling, and (d) competitive noise (Stripe/Tempo, Tether USAT). This type of drawdown is painful but characteristic of early-stage monopoly-calibre businesses trading in cyclically-sensitive markets. The key question is whether the underlying business (supply growth, market share, margin expansion) is intact — and the Q3 2025 data suggests it clearly is.

### 9.2 Distribution Risk: Partner Renegotiation

**The Risk:** Coinbase captures ~50% of USDC reserve income, Binance receives ongoing incentives, and future partners will demand their share. If distribution costs increase beyond the ~67% of reserve income modelled for 2027E, CRCL's net margins compress further.

**The Mitigant:** Four factors limit the distribution risk:

1. *Coinbase's interest is aligned:* USDC reserve income is one of Coinbase's most profitable revenue streams (~$1.5B annualised). Coinbase has no incentive to push Circle into an uneconomic deal — it would damage the golden goose.

2. *Circle on-platform USDC is growing:* The shift from 10% to 14% of supply on Circle's own platform (zero distribution cost) partially offsets distribution expansion elsewhere. Every percentage point shift to on-platform USDC adds ~$35-40M to RLDC at current supply levels.

3. *The "nuclear option" is mutual:* Coinbase could theoretically launch a competing stablecoin, but doing so would (a) destroy the USDC revenue stream during the buildout period, (b) require regulatory approval, and (c) start from zero liquidity against USDC's $76B. The switching costs are prohibitive on both sides.

4. *Diversification reduces concentration:* As Binance, OKX, CPN institutions, and future bank partners grow their share of USDC distribution, Coinbase's relative bargaining power declines. The share of Coinbase in reserve income has already fallen from 55% (2024) to 50% (Q3 2025).

### 9.3 Regulatory Risk

**The Risk:** Despite the GENIUS Act, regulatory execution risk remains. Timelines for rulemaking, OCC charter processing, and international regulatory harmonisation are uncertain. Additionally, future administrations could tighten regulations on stablecoins.

**The Mitigant:** The political economy strongly favours continued stablecoin growth. Stablecoins create demand for U.S. Treasuries (currently top-20 holder and growing), reinforce dollar dominance globally, and generate domestic economic activity. The GENIUS Act passed with bipartisan support. Regulatory reversal would require the U.S. government to actively undermine its own Treasury funding source — politically implausible.

### 9.4 Competitive Risk

**The Risk:** New entrants — Tether's USAT, bank-issued stablecoins, Stripe/Tempo, or an unforeseen BigTech stablecoin — could erode USDC's market share and pricing power.

**The Mitigant:** The stablecoin market is expanding rapidly enough to accommodate multiple winners. Total supply is growing from $260B to a potential $670B by 2027E (and $4T by 2035E). USDC doesn't need to maintain 29% share — even losing 5-10 points of share (to, say, 20%) on a dramatically larger base still produces massive absolute supply growth. The real risk is not market share erosion in an expanding market, but market *contraction* — which would require a sustained crypto bear market AND stalled payments adoption, a low-probability combination.

### 9.5 Operational and Reputational Risks

**Reserve Management:** USDC's reserves are held in U.S. Treasuries and cash at regulated custodians, managed through BlackRock's Circle Reserve Fund. The 2023 SVB event (where Circle had $3.3B in deposits at SVB when it failed) demonstrated both the risk and the resilience: Circle quickly moved assets and restored the peg within 48 hours. The risk of a Treasury default is existential for the global financial system, not specific to Circle — and is therefore non-diversifiable.

**Operational Execution:** Arc blockchain is in testnet with planned commercial launch in 2026. Execution risk is real — a buggy launch, security breach, or failure to attract institutional adoption could undermine the "platform vs. issuer" narrative that justifies a premium valuation. I give low probability to this risk given the calibre of testnet participants (HSBC, Goldman Sachs, Visa, etc.).

**Key Person Risk:** Jeremy Allaire is the visionary behind Circle's positioning at the intersection of crypto, payments, and regulation. His departure would be a meaningful negative signal, though the company's institutional relationships and regulatory licences are not person-dependent.
